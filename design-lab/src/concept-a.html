Concept A

The
Workbook

A two-ink printed workbook. Learning shown as marked-up worksheets, stamped results and overprinted colour.

01Rationale

Fieldwork teaches by making you do the thing, then look at what happened. A printed workbook is the oldest format for exactly that: you write in it, mark it up, stamp it done.

This direction borrows from risograph and printed ephemera: two bright inks that overprint, halftone fades, registration marks, mono labels, big confident grotesque lettering and a reading serif. It is tactile and expressive without being illustrative, and it gives the product a visual grammar: orange means you do something, cobalt means evidence or example, yellow highlight means the note that matters, and ink black means the finished result.

The learning grammar

Do An instruction. Something you act on.

Example A worked sample to compare against.

Result The checked, finished output.

Lower-case grotesque with the o replaced by two misregistered circles. The overlap is the idea: the thing you tried and the thing you checked, printed slightly off, never quite the same. It reads as a wordmark with a built-in symbol, so the circles alone can be the favicon and avatar. In monochrome the circles stay overlapped as a single figure-eight outline. Small sizes drop the overlap offset.

Cobalt on newsprint. Orange stays out of corporate unless it marks an action.

Corporate material uses the same fonts and grammar but narrows the palette to ink and cobalt, drops overprint, and keeps one orange mark per page for the action. It reads as a printed brief from a studio, not as software.

10Strengths, weaknesses, risks

Strengths

  • The most ownable idea: a learning grammar (Do / Example / Result) that the product and the visuals share.
  • Tactile, colourful with a reason, easy to extend to worksheets, stamps and video graphics.
  • Cheap to produce: SVG and CSS, no photography needed.
  • Works at small size: two circles make a clear favicon.

Weaknesses

  • Orange and cobalt are loud. Corporate needs a deliberately restrained version.
  • Risograph aesthetics are currently fashionable and could date.
  • Needs discipline: overprint, stamps and halftone must stay occasional.

Risks

  • Could read as playful or youthful to a conservative agency owner.
  • Orange text fails contrast at small sizes, so it can only be used for blocks and large type.
  • Not yet checked against the real references.
